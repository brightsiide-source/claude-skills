Why divorcing couples choose a cash sale over listing

Listing a house during divorce often means three months of:

  • Coordinating showings between two people who'd rather not coordinate
  • Disputes over staging, paint colors, repair lists, and disclosure language
  • Negotiating offers from buyers, then re-negotiating after inspection
  • Hoping financing doesn't fall through (it does ~15% of the time)
  • Continuing to share mortgage payments while it sits on the market

A cash sale removes all of that. One offer, one signature event, one wire to two accounts. Then it's done.

How New Mexico community property law affects your sale

New Mexico is a community property state. That means assets acquired during the marriage — including the house — are presumed to be jointly owned, and both spouses must sign the deed at closing regardless of whose name is on the title.

This is actually good news for a clean divorce sale: there's no ambiguity about who gets what share. The court (or your settlement agreement) determines the split, and the title company wires accordingly.

How proceeds get split at closing

At the closing table, the title company:

  • Receives our cash payment
  • Pays off the existing mortgage in full
  • Pays off any liens, second mortgages, or HELOCs
  • Pays property tax and HOA prorations
  • Wires the remaining equity per your written instructions — typically 50/50 to two separate accounts, or per your marital settlement agreement

Neither spouse handles funds from the other. Everything goes through the neutral third-party title company. This is one of the cleanest possible financial separations.

What if one of us wants to keep the house?

If one spouse wants to keep the house and refinance the other off the mortgage, our offer can still be useful — it gives you a documented, third-party opinion of the property's cash value. That number can help structure a buyout: the keeping spouse refinances and pays the leaving spouse half the equity at our offer price.
